Civilians who breathed the same types of airborne hazards as deployed military personnel had significantly higher odds of permanent lung disease.

We synthesized 62 independent studies across 11 systematic reviews. Across all airborne hazard types examined, people exposed to the kinds of dust, fumes, and gases common in military deployments had 52% higher odds of developing idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) — a progressive, irreversible scarring of the lungs. Two specific exposure categories showed the strongest evidence: metal dust (+89% odds) and industrial vapors, gases, dusts, and fumes (+75% odds).

Understanding the study

What we studied — and why it matters for veterans

Because tracking what individual service members actually breathed during deployment is nearly impossible — monitoring was too sparse, too delayed, and too incomplete — this research uses decades of civilian occupational studies as the best available scientific analogue. If workers exposed to these same hazards develop lung disease at higher rates, veterans who faced the same exposures — often at even greater intensity and with far less protection — face comparable or greater risk.

Two groups in this analysis
Exposed workers and civilians — people documented to have breathed hazardous airborne substances: metal dust, mixed industrial vapors and fumes (VGDF), silica/stone dust, wood dust, pesticides, or mold. These civilian occupational exposures are the closest scientific analogues to what service members encountered in the Southwest Asia Theater.
Unexposed comparison individuals — people in the same studies without documented airborne hazard exposure, whose lung disease rates define the baseline we compare against.
The central question: Do the airborne hazards common in military deployments — burn pits, desert dust, vehicle exhaust, welding fumes — raise the risk of developing serious, permanent lung disease?
Because direct deployment exposure data is too limited for this analysis, civilian occupational evidence provides the best available scientific basis for estimating veteran risk.

Clinical and policy meaning

What these findings mean for veterans and the VA

The two strongest findings — metal dust (+89% odds) and mixed industrial fumes (+75% odds) — held up under every statistical test we could construct: different models, different assumptions about study quality, outlier removal, and independent software replication. These hazard categories are not incidental to military deployment. They are central to it. Veterans who worked near burn pits, maintained vehicles or aircraft, operated in dust-heavy environments, or were exposed to vector-control spraying were routinely exposed to one or more of these hazard types.

A second finding requires attention: civilian research consistently underestimates risk, because civilian workers often lack precise records of what and how much they were exposed to. This problem is even more pronounced in the military context. The percent-increase figures presented here should be understood as conservative lower bounds on the true risk to veterans.

The Monitoring Gap — Why True Veteran Risk May Be Higher Than These Numbers Suggest
During deployments to the Southwest Asia Theater, environmental monitoring was conducted by a small number of specialists covering vast theater areas. Sampling was periodic — not continuous — and samples were shipped to centralized laboratories for analysis rather than assessed in the field. Even where monitoring occurred, the data are sparse, delayed, and unevenly distributed. This makes it impossible to reconstruct what any individual veteran actually breathed. Without that information, we rely on civilian occupational analogues — which, by how they measure exposure, likely understate the true risk to veterans.
What comes next

Research Gaps and Opportunities

This analysis establishes that the hazard types common in Southwest Asia deployments raise lung disease risk in civilian populations. But it cannot yet answer the follow-up questions most important to veterans: Which specific exposures during which deployments carry the highest individual risk? How long after exposure can disease develop? Who is most vulnerable genetically or biologically? Answering these requires prospective studies that follow deployed service members over time with individual-level exposure records — studies that do not yet exist at scale.

The analysis also raises an early possibility of pre-deployment genetic risk screening. UK Biobank data suggests some individuals may be genetically more susceptible to air pollution-related IPF. This is not yet ready for clinical use, but it points toward a future where preventive measures could be targeted to the most vulnerable before they deploy.

Background
01What is interstitial lung disease — and why should veterans care?

Interstitial lung disease — or ILD — is a family of conditions where the lung tissue becomes permanently inflamed, scarred, or thickened. The most serious form is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, or IPF, which causes the lungs to stiffen progressively, making breathing harder over time. Once the scarring takes hold, it cannot be reversed.

For years, IPF was considered a disease of unknown origin in older people. But research over the past two decades has shown clearly: what you breathe over the course of your life matters. Workers in factories, construction, and agriculture — exposed to metal dust, industrial fumes, silica dust, and other airborne hazards — develop IPF and related lung diseases at significantly higher rates than people who were not exposed.

Veterans deployed to the Southwest Asia Theater from 1990 to 2021 breathed many of those same hazards — often at greater intensity, for longer periods, and with far less protective equipment than the workers studied in civilian research. That connection is the foundation of this study.

02What veterans breathed during deployment

Between 1990 and 2021, hundreds of thousands of service members deployed to Iraq, Afghanistan, and surrounding areas. The air they breathed included a combination of hazards that civilian workers rarely face all at once.

  • Burn pit smoke — open-air burning of nearly every type of waste: plastics, metals, chemicals, and human waste, producing a toxic mixture of particles and gases
  • Desert dust — fine silica-rich particles from frequent sandstorms in one of the world's dustiest regions
  • Vehicle and generator exhaust — diesel fumes from constant convoy operations and base power generation
  • Welding and metalwork fumes — from vehicle, aircraft, and equipment maintenance in field conditions
  • Pesticides — from vector control spraying on bases throughout the theater
These are the exact same categories — metal dust, vapors and gases, silica, pesticides — that civilian research now links to higher odds of lung disease. The difference is that deployed veterans faced them simultaneously, outdoors, for extended tours, without the occupational health controls that apply to civilian workers.

The Evidence
03Why we used civilian research to understand veteran risk

Ideally, we would study veterans directly — measure their individual exposure during deployment and follow them for years to track who develops lung disease. But that kind of study does not exist at scale. Deployment exposure records are incomplete: monitoring was conducted by a small number of specialists covering vast areas, sampling was periodic rather than continuous, and samples were shipped to centralized laboratories rather than analyzed in the field.

Because we cannot reconstruct military exposure directly, we use civilian occupational research as the best available scientific analogue. If factory workers and miners who breathed metal dust and industrial fumes show 89% higher odds of IPF, we have strong scientific grounds for concluding that veterans who breathed the same hazards face comparable — and likely greater — risk. These civilian estimates are the floor, not the ceiling, of veteran risk.

04How we found and analyzed the research

This is what researchers call an umbrella review — a review of systematic reviews. Rather than examining individual studies one at a time, we identified every high-quality review that had already pooled evidence on airborne hazards and interstitial lung disease. We found 11 such reviews, covering 62 independent primary studies, and re-analyzed their data ourselves.

We used a statistical approach called Bayesian meta-analysis, which handles uncertainty in small, variable study sets more honestly than conventional approaches. We then stress-tested every result: multiple independent statistical models, checks for whether removing any single outlier study changed the conclusion, and independent replication of every number in separate software.

The evidence tier assigned to each exposure — Robust, Borderline, Fragile, or Weak — reflects how well each finding survived those tests. Only Robust findings held up under every check we ran.

Key Findings
05The strongest findings: metal dust and industrial fumes

Two exposure types reached our highest evidence tier — Robust. These are findings we are highly confident represent real effects on lung disease risk.

  • Metal dust (adjusted for smoking): 89% higher odds of developing IPF — based on 7 independent studies, with greater than 99.6% probability that the effect is real and not due to chance.
  • Vapors, gases, dusts and fumes (VGDF): 75% higher odds — based on 9 independent studies, with the same statistical confidence.

The overall pooled estimate across all 62 primary studies and all exposure types is a 52% increase in odds of IPF — with greater than 99.9% probability of being real.

These are the same hazard categories veterans encounter during deployment. Burn pits generate vapors, gases, dusts, and fumes. Vehicle and aircraft maintenance involves metal dust and fumes. Both are daily features of life in the Southwest Asia Theater.

06What the sarcoidosis findings mean

Sarcoidosis is another form of lung disease where the immune system forms abnormal clumps of inflamed tissue — called granulomas — most often in the lungs. It is already recognized as a PACT Act presumptive condition for veterans exposed to particulate matter during deployment.

Our analysis found a consistent positive association between airborne hazard exposure and sarcoidosis across silica, pesticides, and mold — individual estimates showed 44% to 67% higher odds. The overall cross-exposure estimate was 86% higher odds, with 99.4% probability of being real.

The sarcoidosis evidence is directionally consistent across every exposure type we examined, but less statistically precise than the IPF evidence. Fewer high-quality studies exist, and there is some evidence of publication bias in the literature. We treat this as a meaningful signal, not yet a confirmed causal link. The desert dust, pesticide, and mold exposures common in the Southwest Asia Theater map directly to the exposure categories linked to sarcoidosis in civilian research.

What This Means
07Why the true risk to veterans may be even higher than these numbers suggest

The percent-increase figures we report — 89% for metal dust, 75% for industrial fumes, 52% overall — are almost certainly conservative estimates of the true risk to veterans. Here is why.

  • Imprecise exposure measurement: In civilian studies, workers often cannot precisely document what they breathed or for how long. This imprecision pushes risk estimates downward toward zero — a well-known statistical problem called non-differential misclassification. Military exposure data is even less precise.
  • Intensity and duration: Deployed service members often worked outdoors, in concentrated exposure environments, for extended tours — conditions that generally exceed what is documented in civilian occupational studies.
  • Simultaneous exposures: Veterans faced burn pit smoke, desert dust, and vehicle exhaust at the same time. Civilian studies typically examine one hazard at a time. The combined effect of simultaneous exposures is not captured in any of these estimates.
The 52% increase in odds we report across all studies should be understood as the floor — not the ceiling — of veteran risk.

08What should happen next — for veterans, the VA, and Congress

This research establishes the scientific foundation for recognizing that airborne hazards common in military deployment environments cause serious lung disease, and provides the evidentiary basis for the PACT Act's presumptive coverage of IPF and sarcoidosis.

  • For the VA: Ensure that veterans with deployment histories receive thorough respiratory screening. IPF and sarcoidosis can progress silently for years — early detection matters.
  • For Congress: Fund prospective cohort studies that follow deployed service members over time with individual-level exposure records. This is the only way to replace civilian analogues with direct military evidence.
  • For researchers: Develop exposure assessment methods that represent the mixed, co-occurring hazards of deployment — burn pit emissions layered on desert dust and vehicle exhaust — rather than studying single substances in isolation.
Establishing a scientific connection between military service and lung disease is not an academic exercise. It is a prerequisite for sound policy and equitable care for the veterans who bore the burden of service.
